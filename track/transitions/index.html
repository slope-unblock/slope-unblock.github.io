Track Transitions

The hardest moments often happen where one track form becomes another: wide to narrow, straight to curved, or ramp to landing.

⚡
Quick answer

Track transitions are moments where width, direction, elevation or visual references change. The safest approach is to enter stable and delay unnecessary follow-up steering until the new surface is readable.

Expect the control context to change

A line that felt centered on one platform can become poor when the next section narrows or turns. Use the visible shape of the destination rather than blindly carrying the old line forward.

Make the important correction before the boundary

When possible, use the wider incoming track to align for the transition. Waiting until the ball reaches the new surface gives you less room to learn how the geometry affects the line.

Observe the first moment after crossing

Give the new section a brief read before stacking inputs. Camera perspective, slope and lateral motion can make the ball appear to need a correction that resolves on its own.

Connect transition quality to the next obstacle

A good transition is one that leaves you ready for what follows. If every post-transition crash begins from the same edge, the entry line—not the next obstacle—may be the real issue.

Apply Track Transitions in a normal run

To test this properly, focus on one feature of the run and leave your keyboard layout, browser size and general steering style unchanged. For Track Transitions, keep the first visible principle in mind: Expect the control context to change. Then use make the important correction before the boundary as the second checkpoint, so the test covers both the initial choice and what follows from it.

Keep the input method constant for several tries. The generated route will vary, so compare decision quality instead of expecting the same geometry. Look for smaller corrections, earlier commitment and a calmer exit as evidence that the technique is transferring between different sections.

A good review ends with one next action. Choose the single change most likely to create more margin on the following attempt, then leave every other habit alone. That discipline turns repeated restarts into a sequence of useful tests rather than identical retries.
