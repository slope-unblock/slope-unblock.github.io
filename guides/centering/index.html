Why Centering Matters

Centering is a positioning habit, not a rule that the middle is always safest; use it to preserve steering options, then leave it when the route requires.

⚡
Quick answer

Centering is useful because it leaves room to move in either direction, but the geometric center is not always the safest line. The real goal is to preserve options for the next visible feature.

Center is a starting position, not a rule

On a wide straight, a neutral middle line gives you similar escape space on both sides. Near a gap, ramp or split route, the useful line may shift well before the feature. Staying “perfectly centered” can become a mistake if the next safe landing is clearly offset.

Judge the line by recovery space

Before steering back toward the middle, look at what comes after the current obstacle. A recovery line is good when it leaves enough width and time for the next decision. If returning to center forces a second sharp move one moment later, hold the safer offset line instead.

Practice neutral recovery

After every successful dodge on a wide early section, try to finish on a calm, controllable line rather than continuing the turn. The drill is not to hit the exact middle pixel; it is to stop the lateral swing. That habit reduces chained corrections later when speed makes every extra input expensive.

Edges sometimes become the correct route

Some generated layouts naturally funnel the ball toward one side. Do not fight the track just to satisfy a centering rule. Use the widest available route, then recover toward a more neutral position only when the geometry gives you time and space.

Apply Why Centering Matters in a normal run

Rather than chasing a score immediately, create a small experiment that shows whether the technique leaves the ball more controllable. For Why Centering Matters, keep the first visible principle in mind: Center is a starting position, not a rule. Then use judge the line by recovery space as the second checkpoint, so the test covers both the initial choice and what follows from it.

Use the immediate feature as the trigger, but evaluate the line one feature later. This prevents a dramatic dodge from being counted as a success when it creates the next crash. The exercise works best when the page's cue can be seen in both the entry and the exit of the sequence.

A good review ends with one next action. Choose the single change most likely to create more margin on the following attempt, then leave every other habit alone. That discipline turns repeated restarts into a sequence of useful tests rather than identical retries.
