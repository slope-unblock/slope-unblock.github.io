After avoiding a hazard, return toward a line that leaves room on both sides when possible. Edge-hugging removes one escape direction before you know what comes next.

Runs 5–7: move your eyes forward

After a crash, name the earliest controllable mistake: late steering, overcorrection, poor landing angle or entering a narrow section off-center. One precise diagnosis is better than ten identical restarts.

Practical takeaways: Slope Beginner Guide: Your First 10 Runs

Beginners improve faster when the first runs have one purpose each. Learn steering response before score, practice returning to usable space after a dodge, then move your eyes farther up the track so decisions begin before the ball reaches danger.

After every crash, identify the earliest controllable cause. A late correction, edge-hugging entry or poor landing angle gives you a concrete practice target; “the track was random” does not.

How to test the advice without wasting runs

Use a controlled set of attempts instead of changing several habits at once. Start with one warm-up run, then repeat the same cue for at least five attempts. Keep the cue simple enough to observe: release the steering key sooner, enter a feature closer to center, or move your eyes farther ahead. Do not judge the idea only by the best score from the block; compare how often the targeted mistake occurred and whether recovery felt less rushed.

When the result is inconsistent, separate route difficulty from execution. A difficult sequence can end a good run even when the earlier decision was reasonable, while a generous sequence can hide weak steering. The goal is to make your decision process more repeatable so that favorable track sections turn into longer runs instead of being wasted by avoidable overcorrection.
