Timing Your Corrections

A correct direction pressed at the wrong time can still cause a crash. Slope rewards early setup and late restraint.

⚡
Quick answer

The best correction is early enough to be small. Wait too long and the same route change requires a longer press, creates more lateral momentum and leaves less time to judge the exit.

Use geometry as the trigger

Start steering because the route tells you a line must change, not because the ball has already reached danger. A visible gap edge, red block or narrowing is a stronger cue than waiting for panic.

Release before the target line if momentum will carry

The ball may continue moving sideways after the key comes up. Learn to release slightly before the visual point you want to occupy, then let momentum finish the movement instead of holding until you are already there.

Avoid stacked corrections

Two or three rapid presses before the first result is visible create uncertainty about which input caused the line. Make one change, read the response and only then add another.

Late saves are feedback

If a run survives only because of a dramatic last-second move, ask what earlier cue was missed. The goal of practice is not to become dependent on emergency corrections; it is to reduce how often they are needed.

Apply Timing Your Corrections in a normal run

Use a short block of runs to translate the idea into a cue you can actually recognize while the ball is moving. For Timing Your Corrections, keep the first visible principle in mind: Use geometry as the trigger. Then use release before the target line if momentum will carry as the second checkpoint, so the test covers both the initial choice and what follows from it.

Keep score in the background during the test. Note entry position, amount of steering and landing stability first. A single high number can come from a favorable route, while a lower-scoring attempt can still reveal a cleaner technique that is more likely to repeat.

A good review ends with one next action. Choose the single change most likely to create more margin on the following attempt, then leave every other habit alone. That discipline turns repeated restarts into a sequence of useful tests rather than identical retries.
